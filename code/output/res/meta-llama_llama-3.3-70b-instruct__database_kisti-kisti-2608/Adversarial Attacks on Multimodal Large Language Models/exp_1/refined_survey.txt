## 1 Introduction

### 1.1 Introduction

The emergence of multimodal large language models has revolutionized the field of artificial intelligence, enabling machines to process and generate content across multiple modalities, including text, images, audio, and video. However, this rapid advancement has also introduced new challenges, particularly in terms of security and robustness. Adversarial attacks, which involve manipulating input data to cause models to produce erroneous or undesirable outputs, have become a significant concern [On the Adversarial Robustness of Multi-Modal Foundation Models]. These attacks can have severe consequences, including compromising model reliability, undermining user trust, and even posing risks to safety and security [Jailbreaking Attack against Multimodal Large Language Model].

Recent studies have highlighted the vulnerability of multimodal large language models to adversarial attacks, including jailbreaking attacks, which can bypass safety mechanisms and induce models to generate harmful or inappropriate content [Visual Adversarial Examples Jailbreak Aligned Large Language Models]. For instance, [On Evaluating Adversarial Robustness of Large Vision-Language Models] demonstrated that vision-language models can be compromised using targeted adversarial examples, compromising their ability to provide accurate and reliable outputs. Similarly, [A Survey on Backdoor Threats in Large Language Models (LLMs): Attacks, Defenses, and Evaluation Methods] emphasized the importance of addressing backdoor threats in large language models, which can be exploited by adversaries to compromise model security.

The importance of understanding and mitigating adversarial attacks on multimodal large language models cannot be overstated. As these models become increasingly ubiquitous in various applications, including natural language processing, computer vision, and human-computer interaction, their vulnerability to attacks can have far-reaching consequences [Large Language Models in Cybersecurity: State-of-the-Art]. Furthermore, the complexity and multimodality of these models make them more challenging to defend against attacks, emphasizing the need for comprehensive and robust defense mechanisms [MM-LLMs: Recent Advances in MultiModal Large Language Models].

Despite the growing concern about adversarial attacks, there is a lack of comprehensive surveys and analyses that provide a thorough understanding of the current state of research in this area. Most existing studies focus on specific aspects of adversarial attacks or defense mechanisms, without providing a broader perspective on the challenges and opportunities in this field [Surveying the MLLM Landscape: A Meta-Review of Current Surveys]. To address this gap, this survey aims to provide a comprehensive overview of adversarial attacks on multimodal large language models, including their types, mechanisms, and evaluation metrics [A Survey on Adversarial Attacks on Multimodal Large Language Models]. By synthesizing existing research and identifying emerging trends and challenges, this survey seeks to contribute to the development of more robust and secure multimodal large language models [Explainable and Interpretable Multimodal Large Language Models: A Comprehensive Survey].

The remainder of this survey will delve into the specifics of adversarial attacks on multimodal large language models, including their classification, methodologies, and evaluation metrics [A Survey on Benchmarks of Multimodal Large Language Models]. We will also discuss the current state of defense mechanisms, including adversarial training, input preprocessing, and detection methods [Baseline Defenses for Adversarial Attacks Against Aligned Language Models]. Furthermore, we will examine the applications and case studies of adversarial attacks on multimodal large language models, highlighting their impact on real-world systems and the need for more comprehensive testing and evaluation protocols [MM-Soc: Benchmarking Multimodal Large Language Models in Social Media Platforms]. By providing a thorough analysis of the current state of research in this area, this survey aims to facilitate the development of more robust and secure multimodal large language models, ultimately contributing to the advancement of artificial intelligence and its applications [Unique Security and Privacy Threats of Large Language Models: A Comprehensive Survey].

The integration of vision and language encoders is typically achieved through attention mechanisms or fusion techniques [Model Composition for Multimodal Large Language Models]. These techniques enable MLLMs to capture complex relationships between different modalities and generate coherent and contextually relevant outputs. ### 2.3 Training Objectives and Methods for Multimodal Large Language Models

The training objectives and methods for multimodal large language models are crucial in enabling these models to effectively process and generate multimodal content, including text, images, and other forms of data. One of the primary training objectives for these models is masked language modeling, which involves masking certain tokens in the input sequence and predicting the masked tokens based on the context [Neural Language Modeling with Visual Features]. This approach has been extended to multimodal settings, where the model is trained to predict masked visual tokens in addition to textual tokens [Multimodal Large Language Models: A Survey]. Another key training objective is next-token prediction, which involves predicting the next token in a sequence given the context [Large Language Models Meet NL2Code: A Survey].

In addition to these objectives, multimodal fusion techniques play a vital role in training multimodal large language models. These techniques involve combining the representations of different modalities, such as text and images, to create a unified representation that can be used for downstream tasks [Macaw-LLM: Multi-Modal Language Modeling with Image, Audio, Video, and Text Integration]. One popular approach for multimodal fusion is early fusion, which involves combining the representations of different modalities at an early stage of the model [From CLIP to DINO: Visual Encoders Shout in Multi-modal Large Language Models]. Another approach is late fusion, which involves combining the representations of different modalities at a later stage of the model [ChatBridge: Bridging Modalities with Large Language Model as a Language Catalyst].

Recent studies have also explored the use of intermediate fusion techniques, which involve combining the representations of different modalities at intermediate stages of the model [MoME: Mixture of Multimodal Experts for Generalist Multimodal Large Language Models]. These techniques have been shown to be effective in capturing the complex interactions between different modalities and improving the overall performance of the model [Visual Prompting in Multimodal Large Language Models: A Survey]. Furthermore, the use of attention mechanisms has been found to be effective in multimodal fusion, as it allows the model to focus on the most relevant features of each modality when combining them [Model Composition for Multimodal Large Language Models].

The training methods for multimodal large language models also involve the use of large-scale datasets that contain a diverse range of multimodal content [A Survey on Multimodal Benchmarks: In the Era of Large AI Models]. These datasets are typically constructed by combining existing datasets from different modalities and annotating them with labels that indicate the relationships between the different modalities [A Survey on Evaluation of Multimodal Large Language Models]. The models are then trained on these datasets using a combination of supervised and self-supervised learning techniques [MM-LLMs: Recent Advances in MultiModal Large Language Models].

In terms of evaluation metrics, multimodal large language models are typically evaluated using a combination of metrics that assess their performance on different modalities and tasks [Eagle: Exploring The Design Space for Multimodal LLMs with Mixture of Encoders]. These metrics may include measures of accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score, as well as metrics that assess the model's ability to generate coherent and contextually relevant multimodal content [How Multimodal LLMs Solve Image Tasks: A Lens on Visual Grounding, Task Reasoning, and Answer Decoding]. Recent studies have also highlighted the importance of evaluating multimodal large language models using metrics that assess their robustness and generalizability to different datasets and tasks [Seeing is Understanding: Unlocking Causal Attention into Modality-Mutual Attention for Multimodal LLMs].

Overall, the training objectives and methods for multimodal large language models are critical in enabling these models to effectively process and generate multimodal content. By combining different modalities and using a range of training objectives and methods, these models can capture the complex interactions between different modalities and improve their overall performance [LaVi: Efficient Large Vision-Language Models via Internal Feature Modulation]. As the field continues to evolve, it is likely that we will see the development of new training objectives and methods that can further improve the performance of multimodal large language models [MHA2MLA-VLM: Enabling DeepSeek's Economical Multi-Head Latent Attention across Vision-Language Models].

## 3 Types and Mechanisms of Adversarial Attacks

### 3.1 Classification of Adversarial Attacks

The classification of adversarial attacks on multimodal large language models is a crucial aspect of understanding and mitigating these threats. Adversarial attacks can be broadly categorized into two primary types: targeted and untargeted attacks [On Evaluating Adversarial Perturbations for Sequence-to-Sequence Models]. Targeted attacks are designed to cause the model to produce a specific, incorrect output, and can be further divided into subcategories based on their goals, such as misclassification or data extraction [Use of LLMs for Illicit Purposes: Threats, Prevention Measures, and Vulnerabilities]. Untargeted attacks, on the other hand, aim to cause the model to produce any incorrect output, without a specific goal in mind, and can be used to evaluate the model's overall robustness [Exploring Vulnerabilities and Protections in Large Language Models: A Survey].

Model evasion attacks are a type of targeted attack that involves manipulating the input to evade the model's detection or classification capabilities [Jailbreaking Attack against Multimodal Large Language Model]. These attacks can be used to bypass security mechanisms, such as content filters or spam detectors, and can have significant consequences in applications where security is critical [Security and Privacy Challenges of Large Language Models: A Survey]. Model extraction attacks, another type of targeted attack, involve extracting sensitive information from the model, such as its parameters or training data [A Survey on Backdoor Threats in Large Language Models (LLMs): Attacks, Defenses, and Evaluation Methods]. Data poisoning attacks, a type of untargeted attack, involve manipulating the training data to compromise the model's performance or robustness [A Survey on Multimodal Benchmarks: In the Era of Large AI Models].

The goals of adversarial attacks can vary widely, depending on the motivations of the attacker. Some attacks may be designed to compromise the model's accuracy or robustness, while others may aim to extract sensitive information or bypass security mechanisms [Unique Security and Privacy Threats of Large Language Models: A Comprehensive Survey]. Understanding the goals and mechanisms of adversarial attacks is essential for developing effective defense mechanisms and evaluating the robustness of models against various types of attacks [Surveying the MLLM Landscape: A Meta-Review of Current Surveys].

Recent studies have shown that multimodal large language models are vulnerable to a wide range of adversarial attacks, including those that exploit the model's visual or textual inputs [Probing the Robustness of Vision-Language Pretrained Models: A Multimodal Adversarial Attack Approach]. For example, [On the Adversarial Robustness of Multi-Modal Foundation Models] demonstrated that multimodal models can be compromised using adversarial images, while [Visual Adversarial Examples Jailbreak Aligned Large Language Models] showed that visual adversarial examples can be used to jailbreak aligned large language models. These findings highlight the need for robust defense mechanisms that can protect against a wide range of adversarial attacks [Robust-LLaVA: On the Effectiveness of Large-Scale Robust Image Encoders for Multi-modal Large Language Models].

In addition to understanding the types and goals of adversarial attacks, it is also essential to evaluate the effectiveness of defense mechanisms against these attacks [A Survey on Evaluation of Multimodal Large Language Models]. This can involve assessing the model's robustness against various types of attacks, as well as evaluating the performance of defense mechanisms, such as adversarial training or input preprocessing [Baseline Defenses for Adversarial Attacks Against Aligned Language Models]. Recent studies have shown that defense mechanisms can be effective in mitigating the impact of adversarial attacks, but that there is still a need for more robust and effective defense strategies [MM-LLMs: Recent Advances in MultiModal Large Language Models].

In conclusion, the classification of adversarial attacks on multimodal large language models is a critical aspect of understanding and mitigating these threats. By understanding the types and goals of adversarial attacks, as well as the mechanisms and effectiveness of defense mechanisms, researchers and practitioners can develop more robust and secure models that can protect against a wide range of adversarial attacks [Jailbreak Attacks and Defenses against Multimodal Generative Models: A Survey]. Future research should focus on developing more effective defense mechanisms, as well as evaluating the robustness of models against various types of attacks [Survey of Adversarial Robustness in Multimodal Large Language Models]. This will require a comprehensive understanding of the types and goals of adversarial attacks, as well as the mechanisms and effectiveness of defense mechanisms [A Survey on Benchmarks of Multimodal Large Language Models].

### 3.2 Methodologies for Generating Adversarial Examples

The generation of adversarial examples is a crucial step in understanding and evaluating the robustness of multimodal large language models, as it enables researchers to identify potential vulnerabilities and develop effective defense mechanisms. Building on the classification of adversarial attacks discussed earlier, the generation of adversarial examples can be used to assess the model's robustness against various types of attacks, including targeted and untargeted attacks. In the context of multimodal large language models, adversarial examples can be generated using a variety of methodologies, including gradient-based methods and gradient-free methods.

Gradient-based methods, such as the fast gradient sign method [A Survey on Attacks on Large Vision–Language Models: Resources, Advances, and Future Trends], are widely used for generating adversarial examples. These methods leverage the model's gradients to optimize the adversarial example, often resulting in highly effective attacks that can be computationally efficient. For instance, the fast gradient sign method can be used to generate adversarial examples for image classification tasks by maximizing the loss function with respect to the input image. However, gradient-based methods can be limited by their reliance on model gradients, which may not always be accessible or reliable.

In contrast, gradient-free methods, such as genetic algorithms and evolutionary strategies [MM-LLMs: Recent Advances in MultiModal Large Language Models], do not rely on gradient information and can be used when the model's gradients are not accessible. These methods can be more computationally intensive than gradient-based methods but offer an alternative approach for generating adversarial examples. For example, genetic algorithms can be used to search for adversarial examples by iteratively evolving a population of candidate inputs. While gradient-free methods can be effective, they may require significant computational resources and may not always converge to optimal solutions.

Another approach for generating adversarial examples is to use hybrid methods that combine gradient-based and gradient-free methods. These methods can leverage the strengths of each approach to improve the success rate of adversarial examples. The choice of methodology depends on the specific application, the type of attack being considered, and the availability of model gradients.

The evaluation of adversarial examples is also an essential aspect of studying adversarial attacks on multimodal large language models. Evaluation metrics, such as attack success rate and model accuracy under attack, can be used to assess the effectiveness of adversarial examples [A Survey on Evaluation of Multimodal Large Language Models]. These metrics can provide insights into the robustness of a model and the potential vulnerabilities that can be exploited by attackers. As discussed in the following section, the evaluation of adversarial attacks is crucial for understanding the model's robustness and developing effective defense mechanisms.

In conclusion, the generation of adversarial examples is a critical component in the study of adversarial attacks on multimodal large language models. Various methodologies, including gradient-based and gradient-free methods, can be used to generate adversarial examples, each with their strengths and limitations. The evaluation of adversarial examples is also crucial, and different evaluation metrics can provide different insights into the robustness of a model. As the field of multimodal large language models continues to evolve, it is essential to continue to develop and evaluate new methodologies for generating adversarial examples and to improve our understanding of the vulnerabilities of these models. Future research directions may include the development of more robust models that can withstand adversarial attacks, as well as the exploration of new applications and domains where multimodal large language models can be used [A Survey on Benchmarks of Multimodal Large Language Models].

### 3.3 Evaluation Metrics for Adversarial Attacks

The evaluation of adversarial attacks on multimodal large language models is a crucial aspect of understanding their vulnerability and robustness. Various metrics have been proposed to assess the effectiveness of these attacks, including attack success rate, model accuracy under attack, and robustness metrics [MM-LLMs: Recent Advances in MultiModal Large Language Models]. The attack success rate measures the percentage of successful attacks, providing a direct indication of the attack's effectiveness and the model's vulnerability. Model accuracy under attack, on the other hand, evaluates the model's performance under adversarial conditions, helping to understand its robustness and ability to maintain accuracy in the face of attacks.

Robustness metrics, such as the robustness score or certification bounds, offer a more comprehensive view of the model's ability to withstand adversarial perturbations, beyond just accuracy or attack success rate [A Survey on Benchmarks of Multimodal Large Language Models]. These metrics are essential for assessing the model's reliability and security in various applications. Furthermore, [Visual Prompting in Multimodal Large Language Models: A Survey] highlights the importance of evaluating the model's performance under different attack scenarios, including targeted and untargeted attacks.

In addition to these metrics, [A Survey on Evaluation of Multimodal Large Language Models] proposes the use of adversarial training to improve the model's robustness. Adversarial training involves training the model on adversarial examples to enhance its ability to withstand attacks. 

The choice of evaluation metric depends on the specific application and the type of attack being considered. For instance, [Multimodal Large Language Models for Text-rich Image Understanding: A Comprehensive Review] suggests that the attack success rate is a suitable metric for evaluating the effectiveness of attacks on text-rich image understanding tasks. On the other hand, [A Survey on Evaluation of Multimodal Large Language Models] recommends using a combination of metrics, including model accuracy under attack and robustness metrics, to provide a comprehensive evaluation of the model's robustness.

Recent studies have also explored the use of explainability techniques to understand the model's behavior under adversarial attacks. For example, [Explaining Multi-modal Large Language Models by Analyzing their Vision Perception] proposes the use of saliency maps to identify the regions of the input that are most relevant to the model's predictions. 

In conclusion, the evaluation of adversarial attacks on multimodal large language models requires a comprehensive approach that considers multiple metrics and techniques. By selecting suitable evaluation metrics and using techniques such as adversarial training and explainability, researchers can gain a deeper understanding of the model's robustness and vulnerability to adversarial attacks. Future research should focus on developing more effective evaluation metrics and techniques, as well as improving the robustness of multimodal large language models to adversarial attacks [Surveying the MLLM Landscape: A Meta-Review of Current Surveys]. This will be crucial for ensuring the reliability and security of these models in various applications, including natural language processing, computer vision, and human-computer interaction.

### 3.4 Defense Mechanisms Against Adversarial Attacks

The development of effective defense mechanisms against adversarial attacks on multimodal large language models is a critical area of research, given the potential consequences of such attacks on model reliability and security. As [MLLM-Protector] notes, multimodal large language models are susceptible to malicious attacks through visual inputs, highlighting the need for robust defense strategies. Building on the evaluation metrics and techniques discussed earlier, such as attack success rate, model accuracy under attack, and robustness metrics, defense mechanisms can be designed to enhance model robustness and reliability. One approach to mitigating these attacks is adversarial training, which involves training the model on adversarial examples to improve its robustness. This method has been shown to enhance model performance under attack, but it can be computationally expensive and may not provide comprehensive protection against all types of attacks.

In addition to adversarial training, other defense mechanisms, such as input preprocessing and detection methods, can be employed to identify and mitigate adversarial examples. Input preprocessing techniques, including data normalization, feature extraction, and dimensionality reduction, can reduce the impact of adversarial perturbations, but their effectiveness depends on the specific attack and model architecture. Detection methods, including statistical methods and machine learning-based approaches, can also be used to identify potential adversarial inputs, but may introduce additional computational overhead and require careful tuning of hyperparameters. Recent studies have explored the use of mixture-of-experts (MoE) architectures to improve the robustness of multimodal large language models [MoME]. By routing task-specific tokens through specialized experts, MoE models can reduce the risk of adversarial attacks while maintaining model performance.

The development of effective defense mechanisms against adversarial attacks on multimodal large language models is an ongoing challenge, and the evaluation of these mechanisms is crucial to ensuring their effectiveness. As [A Survey on Multimodal Benchmarks] highlights, standardized benchmarks are needed to facilitate comparison and improvement of different approaches. Furthermore, the trade-off between model robustness and performance on benign inputs must be carefully considered, as overly defensive models may compromise their ability to perform well on legitimate tasks. Emerging trends in defense mechanisms against adversarial attacks include the use of explainable AI techniques to provide insights into model vulnerabilities [Explainable and Interpretable Multimodal Large Language Models] and the development of more efficient and scalable defense methods [EE-MLLM]. Additionally, research has focused on adapting defense mechanisms to specific application domains, such as autonomous driving [A Survey on Multimodal Large Language Models for Autonomous Driving], where the consequences of adversarial attacks can be particularly severe.

In conclusion, the development of effective defense mechanisms against adversarial attacks on multimodal large language models is a complex and multifaceted challenge.
