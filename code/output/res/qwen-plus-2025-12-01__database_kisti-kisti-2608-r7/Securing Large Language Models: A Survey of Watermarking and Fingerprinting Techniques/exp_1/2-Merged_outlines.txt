Title: Securing Large Language Models: A Survey of Watermarking and Fingerprinting Techniques

Section 1: Introduction  
Description 1: This section introduces the foundational motivation, scope, and intellectual framing of watermarking and fingerprinting as essential technical mechanisms for securing large language models across their lifecycle—from training and fine-tuning to deployment and adaptation—while distinguishing these approaches from broader privacy, safety, and robustness paradigms.  
1. The escalating economic, legal, and societal imperatives for model and output provenance, driven by high computational investment in model development, growing regulatory mandates for synthetic content transparency, and widespread concerns about misinformation, academic misconduct, and unauthorized model replication.  
2. Conceptual clarification of core terminology: watermarking as an active, owner-controlled process of embedding verifiable signals into model outputs or internal representations, and fingerprinting as a passive, forensic technique for uniquely identifying model instances based on intrinsic behavioral, structural, or statistical signatures without requiring prior embedding.  
3. A roadmap of the survey’s analytical architecture—highlighting its dual focus on technical rigor (e.g., formal security properties, adversarial resilience, architectural compatibility) and socio-technical integration (e.g., governance implications, interoperability, human-centered evaluation)—and its deliberate departure from siloed treatments toward a unified framework for intellectual property protection in generative artificial intelligence.

Section 2: Foundations and Taxonomy of Watermarking and Fingerprinting Paradigms  
Description 2: This section establishes a rigorous, principle-driven classification system that transcends implementation details to organize techniques along theoretically grounded dimensions—including embedding locus, detection modality, threat model assumptions, and formal security guarantees—enabling precise cross-method comparison and revealing deep structural parallels across historically disparate domains.  
1. A tripartite functional taxonomy based on signal embedding locus: output-space watermarking (modifying generated token sequences or probability distributions), parameter-space watermarking and fingerprinting (embedding or extracting identifiers from model weights, attention matrices, or low-rank adaptations), and behavior-space fingerprinting (inferring provenance from input-output mappings, activation patterns, or decision-boundary responses under black-box access).  
2. Formal characterization of detection modalities—white-box (requiring full model weight access), gray-box (leveraging logits, intermediate activations, or architectural knowledge), and black-box (relying solely on query-response behavior)—with explicit analysis of how each modality constrains adversary capabilities and shapes verification feasibility in real-world deployment contexts.  
3. Information-theoretic and cryptographic foundations defining core security properties: detectability (statistical distinguishability from unmarked outputs), robustness (resistance to semantic-preserving transformations such as paraphrasing, translation, or summarization), unforgeability (infeasibility of injecting counterfeit watermarks without secret keys), and collusion resistance (preservation of uniqueness when multiple users or models interact).

Section 3: Technical Architectures for Output-Level Watermarking  
Description 3: This section surveys algorithmic frameworks that embed detectable yet imperceptible signals directly into the text sequences generated by large language models during inference, emphasizing design trade-offs among statistical power, linguistic fidelity, and compatibility with autoregressive decoding pipelines.  
1. Sampling-based watermarking methods that bias next-token selection probabilities using pseudorandom vocabulary partitioning (e.g., green-list sampling), entropy-aware perturbation thresholds, and Gumbel-softmax–guided interventions—analyzing their dependence on tokenizer behavior, temperature sensitivity, and vulnerability to character-level attacks like homoglyph substitution or whitespace manipulation.  
2. Cryptographic and learning-based watermarking schemes that embed publicly verifiable digital signatures via rejection sampling or error-correcting codes, and co-train large language models with differentiable watermark decoders to support open-model deployment and multi-bit payload encoding (e.g., user identity, timestamp, model version) without compromising fluency or coherence.  
3. Context-aware and adaptive watermarking architectures that dynamically modulate embedding strength based on generation uncertainty, syntactic structure, or instruction-following fidelity—ensuring stronger watermarking in high-risk segments (e.g., factual claims, code blocks) while minimizing interference in low-entropy or safety-critical contexts.

Section 4: Model-Centric Fingerprinting and Parameter-Space Watermarking  
Description 4: This section examines techniques that bind ownership, integrity, or provenance directly to the large language model itself—either by extracting intrinsic identifiers from its parameters or behavior, or by embedding persistent signals during training or fine-tuning—enabling verification without reliance on output modification or external infrastructure.  
1. Weight-space fingerprinting methods leveraging singular value decomposition, eigenvalue spectra, or attention weight manifold geometry to generate transformation-invariant identifiers resilient to quantization, pruning, fine-tuning, and model merging.  
2. Backdoor-based and trigger-free watermarking strategies that imprint deterministic, secret-key–dependent response patterns through lightweight fine-tuning, low-rank adaptation, or neuron-level perturbations—ensuring zero-performance-cost embedding and resistance to human or automated scrutiny of trigger inputs.  
3. Training-free behavioral fingerprinting paradigms that exploit stable statistical biases in token selection, repetition patterns, calibration errors, or layer-wise activation sparsity profiles across diverse prompts—enabling black-box identification of model families, versions, or fine-tuned variants without access to weights or retraining.

Section 5: Robustness, Adversarial Vulnerabilities, and Attack Taxonomies  
Description 5: This section provides a systematic synthesis of empirically validated and theoretically grounded attack vectors targeting watermarking and fingerprinting systems, organized by adversary capability (black-box vs. white-box), operational goal (removal, spoofing, false attribution), and attack surface (output, parameter, or behavior space).  
1. Scrubbing and removal attacks—including post-generation paraphrasing, synonym substitution, machine translation, recursive rewriting, and fine-tuning–based erasure—that degrade detection efficacy while preserving semantic coherence, fluency, and task performance.  
2. Spoofing, forgery, and piggyback attacks that synthesize watermarked-looking outputs without secret keys, inject malicious content alongside legitimate watermarks, or exploit knowledge distillation to forge ownership claims—undermining liability assignment and enabling false-flag operations.  
3. Structural and systemic attacks targeting fingerprint integrity—including model merging, gradient inversion, selective unlearning, mismatched dataset fine-tuning, and collusion-based fingerprint neutralization—that eliminate embedded identifiers without full retraining or expose vulnerabilities in radioactivity assumptions.

Section 6: Evaluation Frameworks, Benchmarks, and Real-World Deployment Constraints  
Description 6: This section critically reviews empirical validation practices for watermarking and fingerprinting, exposing methodological gaps between laboratory metrics and fielded system requirements—and proposing principles for fair, reproducible, multidimensional assessment aligned with operational realities.  
1. Standardized evaluation dimensions including detection accuracy (true positive and false positive rates), watermark survival rate under realistic perturbations, payload capacity (bits per token), perceptual quality degradation (perplexity, BLEU, human fluency ratings), and alignment preservation (truthfulness, safety, helpfulness).  
2. Emerging benchmark initiatives—such as JailbreakBench for adversarial robustness, Dual Attribution Benchmark for multi-provenance watermarking, and MUSE for unlearning-aware evaluation—that co-evaluate watermarking, fingerprinting, and detection under unified threat models spanning languages, domains, and editing intensities.  
3. Operational constraints in production environments—including latency overhead, memory footprint of watermark decoders, compatibility with speculative decoding and streaming inference, interference with safety guardrails and moderation layers, and integration challenges in confidential computing or edge-device deployments.

Section 7: Cross-Domain Extensions, Governance Integration, and Socio-Technical Implications  
Description 7: This section moves beyond isolated technical mechanisms to examine how watermarking and fingerprinting interact with heterogeneous modalities, system architectures, regulatory frameworks, and human stakeholders—emphasizing interoperability, ethical accountability, and ecosystem-level viability.  
1. Multimodal and cross-modal extensions for vision-language models, audio-language models, diffusion-based generators, and code-generation systems—addressing novel challenges of joint-modality signal embedding, perceptual invisibility, functional correctness preservation, and cross-modal consistency binding.  
2. Governance-aware design principles for regulatory compliance—including auditability of watermark injection, verifiable inference protocols for regulatory reporting, transparency obligations under artificial intelligence legislation, and admissibility of watermark evidence in intellectual property disputes.  
3. Socio-technical tensions and equity considerations—including risks of false attribution and collateral harm, disparities in detection performance across linguistic registers and low-resource languages, dual-use potential for surveillance or exclusionary access control, and incentive misalignment across model providers, platform operators, and end users.

Section 8: Conclusion  
Description 8: This section synthesizes key insights, unresolved tensions, and forward-looking research trajectories emerging from the survey—distilling lessons learned about the fundamental limits, practical trade-offs, and interdisciplinary dependencies of watermarking and fingerprinting as pillars of large language model security.  
1. Summary of dominant technical trends—including the convergence of statistical watermarking with cryptographic primitives, the rise of behavior-space fingerprinting for black-box verification, and the growing emphasis on multimodal and system-level integrity rather than text-only solutions.  
2. Critical outstanding challenges: the absence of formal cryptographic security proofs for watermark unforgeability under realistic threat models; the lack of longitudinal studies on watermark persistence across iterative fine-tuning, continual learning, and model versioning; and the insufficient integration of human-centered evaluation into technical validation pipelines.  
3. A call for holistic, co-designed assurance frameworks—where watermarking and fingerprinting are not treated as standalone add-ons but integrated components of responsible artificial intelligence governance stacks, aligned with privacy-enhancing technologies, formal verification, explainability, and environmental sustainability objectives.
