# Large Language Model based Multi-Agent Systems: Architectures, Collective Intelligence, and Emerging Frontiers

## 1 Introduction
Description: This section establishes the foundational context for the survey by tracing the evolution of intelligent systems from classical multi-agent frameworks to the contemporary paradigm of large language model based multi-agent systems. It articulates the unique capabilities that large language models bring to agent design, defines the scope and core terminology of the field, and positions this survey relative to existing literature.
1. Historical Evolution from Classical to Language-Driven Agents: Traces the progression from rule-based and reinforcement learning driven multi-agent systems to large language model powered agents, highlighting the paradigm shift from predefined state-action spaces and explicit coordination protocols to flexible, natural language mediated, and knowledge-rich interactions enabled by pretrained language models.
2. Motivation and Unique Capabilities: Examines the rationale for adopting multiple large language model agents instead of a monolithic single model, focusing on how collaboration overcomes limitations such as finite context windows, individual hallucinations or biases, and lack of specialized knowledge through workflow division and the use of their inherent reasoning, planning, and in-context learning capabilities.
3. Distinguishing System Properties: Delineates the core defining features of large language model based multi-agent systems, separating them from both a single agent and a simple ensemble of models, including the presence of distinct specified roles for agents, communication and various types of interaction, and the capability for emergent collective behavior that surpasses any single component's abilities.
4. Scope and Survey Positioning: Outlines the scientific scope, the organizing structure of the survey, and the main strands of existing research, situating the work in relation to prior surveys that tend to focus on a single aspect—like memory or evaluation—rather than providing a comprehensive and unified framework that links, architecture, dynamics, applications, and societal challenges.

## 2 Architectural Design of Agents and Multi-Agent Systems
Description: This section provides a systematic treatment of the architectural and design choices for constructing individual agents and orchestrating them into multi-agent systems. It deconstructs the internal cognitive modules of a single agent, analyzes the different system-level topological configurations, and evaluates the critical design decisions that shape communication, scalability, and system resilience.
1. Profiling, Persona, and Task Specialization: Explores the methods for defining an agent's role and identity through natural language profiling, including the assignment of distinct professional roles, personality traits, or specific functions, and examines both static and dynamic approaches to role composition that facilitate the division of complex tasks into subtasks handled by specialized agents.
2. Agent Memory Architectures and Knowledge Management: Discusses the core mechanisms for memory construction in individual agents and the system as a whole, spanning short-term working memory for immediate context, long-term episodic and semantic memory modules with storage and retrieval, and the formation of shared memory repositories that collectively track the group's learned knowledge and environmental state.
3. System Organization and Coordination Topologies: Explores the primary multi-agent structural arrangements, including centralized, decentralized, hierarchical, and flat configurations, detailing the flow of control and data, and analyzing the inherent trade-offs among these designs regarding system scalability, fault tolerance, and the overhead required for coordinating the constituent agents.
4. Hybrid, Modular, and Design-Space Trade-offs: Examines the main system-level design patterns to compose agents in a flexible way, including hybrid approaches and platform-based compositions of "registries" that dynamically mix human users and heterogeneous artificial intelligence systems, and analyzes the fundamental trade-offs between system performance, latency, communication cost, and the robustness of the agent network.

### 2.1 Agent Profiling and Persona Engineering
Description: This subsection examines the methods and frameworks for defining agent identity, roles, and behavioral characteristics within multi-agent systems. It covers the spectrum from simple role assignment through natural language prompting to sophisticated personality modeling using psychological frameworks, and analyzes the challenges of maintaining persona consistency in collaborative settings.
1. Natural Language Role Specification and Professional Personas: Examines approaches for assigning specialized roles—such as software engineers, analysts, or domain experts—via natural language prompts and system messages, and explores how these role definitions shape agent behavior, task allocation, and the division of cognitive labor.
2. Personality Modeling and Psychological Trait Representation: Analyzes techniques for imbuing agents with distinct personality traits drawing on established psychological frameworks such as the Big Five, HEXACO, and MBTI, including methods like specialized LoRA experts, behavioral elicitation, and structured profiling that go beyond simple attribute assignment to achieve more faithful and nuanced personalities.
3. Persona Consistency and Dynamic Adaptation: Investigates the challenges of maintaining coherent personas across extended interactions and dynamic environments, addressing issues of persona drift, conformity pressure during multi-agent discussions, and techniques for building agents that can evolve their profiles based on feedback and environmental cues without catastrophic forgetting.
4. Evaluation and Verification of Agent Personas: Describes methods for assessing persona faithfulness and quality, including automatic metrics, user studies, and the use of external evaluator models to verify role adherence, detect personality instability, and ensure that the assigned personas reliably shape the interaction.

### 2.2 Agent Memory Architectures and Knowledge Management
Description: This subsection addresses the design of memory systems for individual agents and the collective, drawing on cognitive science to structure memory into distinct functional components. It explores how memory is encoded, stored, and retrieved across different timescales and how individual memory modules are expanded into a shared repository that informs the group's actions.
- Individual Memory Structures and Cognitive Frameworks: Covers the configuration of memory in a single agent, including short-term and working memory for immediate context, long-term episodic memory for past experiences, and semantic memory for factual knowledge, and examines implementations using data formats such as knowledge graphs, vector databases, and structured natural language summaries.
- Collective and Shared Memory Systems: Examines how agents can coordinate through a shared, distributed memory that tracks the group's progress, common knowledge, and environment state, and how this central repository facilitates collaboration, task handoffs, and avoids redundancy in multi-agent workflows.
- Memory Operations, Retrieval, and Integration: Details the operational procedures of the memory lifecycle—encoding, consolidation, retrieval, and updating—along with the different retrieval strategies, including dense retrieval, retrieval-augmented generation, and graph-based traversal, that integrate past experience with the planning and decision-making of the agent.
- Advanced Memory Management for Long-Horizon Tasks: Addresses the unique challenges of and solutions for handling memory in complex long-horizon environments, including the use of subgoals as memory chunks, hierarchical working memory, and other mechanisms that prioritize and summarize information from long interaction histories.

### 2.3 System Architectures and Organizational Topologies
Description: This subsection describes the system-level structural design patterns for orchestrating multiple agents. It provides a systematic overview of the canonical topological configurations—centralized, decentralized, hierarchical, and flat—and analyzes the crucial structural designs that determine the flow of control, data, and influence, relating them to system-level attributes like scalability, resilience, and communication overhead.
- Centralized and Hierarchical Coordination: Discusses the hub-and-spoke model where an orchestrator or lead agent decomposes tasks, distributes sub-tasks, and its internal and its internal mechanisms, emphasizing the control flow, and the benefits of the primary directives with the key limitations of a single point of failure regarding scalability and the coordinator's context windows.
- Decentralized and Flat Organizational Models: Divides into a peer-to-peer arrangement of agents where interactions are not centrally coordinated, and examines the process by which agents share information through direct communication, self-organize, and act without a central authority; discusses its enhanced characteristics of resilience and scalability, but the high communication costs required to reach consensus.
- Dynamic Reconfiguration and Self-Organization: Depicts the more recent progression beyond static organizational structures, allowing for the agent network to change dynamically during a task, including the agent's recruitment of new members, the altering of the communication hierarchy, or the removal of unhelpful agents based on current state, including the inference-time, and dynamics derived from optimization objectives.
- Design Trade-offs: Coordination Cost, Fault Tolerance, System Scalability: Provides a comparative analysis of the architectural topologies, operationalizing them on trade-offs that are not both possible in terms of latency, communication overhead, fault-tolerance to component failure, and ease of scalability, and provides a structural framing to aid the designer in choosing the appropriate architecture for a given task domain.

### 2.4 Integration with Tools, Modular Frameworks, and External Environments
Description: This subsection focuses on the relationship between the agent system and its external environment, focusing on the architectural patterns for incorporating external tools, application programming interfaces (APIs), and heterogeneous components. It deals with the modular design and abstraction layers that allow for agents to operate with a wide range of non-LLM functions and specialized systems.
- Tool Integration and Application Programming Interfaces (APIs): Covers the mechanisms for expanding an agent's capabilities beyond natural language, including methods for invoking external APIs, code execution environments, and web search functionality; it also explores the API-first design principles that prioritize programmatic interfaces over UI interactions for improved reliability and latency.
- Human-Agent and Mixed-initiative Architectures: Examines the interplay between human users and AI agents within a structured framework, including the orchestration of agents by humans in the design and critique phases, and the architectural support for human-in-the-loop and human-on-the-loop governance and routing, which incorporate human intents and mental models.
- Agent Operating Systems and Infrastructure Platforms: Outlines the conceptual and practical infrastructure for multi-agent systems, describing "operating systems" for agents that propose resource allocation, scheduling, context switching, and concurrent execution, as well as the protocols and registries that enable interoperability of heterogeneous third-party agents.
- Composition and Design Spaces of Modular Systems: Provides a synthesis of the trade-offs in composing the system from a diverse set of modules. This explores frameworks that combine LLM components with procedural logic, non-LLM experts, and knowledge bases within a single-agent or multi-agent workflow. Focus on how these approaches help to manage the limitations and hallucinations of the LLM and provide specialized performance, while balancing cost vs. effectiveness, latency, and robustness.

## 3 Communication, Interaction, and Coordination Mechanisms
Description: This section focuses on the "social" layer of multi-agent systems, detailing the protocols, structures, and strategies that govern the exchange of information, the coordination of actions, and the resolution of conflicting objectives. This section goes into depth on the different communicative paradigms of cooperation, debate, and negotiation, evolving the central role of natural language in generating collective intelligence.
1. Communication Protocols and Languages: Analyzes the design of agent messaging systems, spanning open-ended natural dialogue and structured data formats, and investigates more sophisticated information-sharing practices such as sharing a "belief state" in a mathematical embedding to convey unspoken inference, and evaluates how these methods affect the fidelity, efficiency, and expressiveness of the interactions.
2. Debate, Negotiation, and Consensus-Building: Details common social interaction paradigms, including structured multi-round debate, counter-argument, and recursive reasoning for improved self-consistency, and the use of voting, hierarchical review, or mediation strategies for conflict management and the consolidation of distributed knowledge into a single final output.
3. Structural and Dynamic Organizational Strategies: Distinguishes between static team configurations based on pre-defined role assignment, and organizational structures where the agents can self-organize their communication hierarchy, choose their collaborators dynamically, or appear to be assigned to their task based on changing requirements during execution.
4. The Dynamics of Group Deliberation and its Emergence: Explores the pattern of coordination and group reasoning, where the exchange of adversarial arguments, the iterative refinement of plans, and the sharing of high-level sub-goals among agents implement the philosophy of a "society of mind," illustrating how collaboration between different modules enables reasoning to be a novel, emergent property of the system.

### 3.1 Communication Protocols and Information Exchange Paradigms
Description: This subsection examines the foundational communication layer of LLM-based multi-agent systems, analyzing how agents exchange information through different protocols, message formats, and representational strategies. It contrasts natural language dialogue with structured symbolic communication and embedding-based approaches, highlighting their respective trade-offs in expressiveness, efficiency, and information fidelity for enabling effective collective reasoning.
1. Natural Language Dialogue and Structured Protocol Design: Explores the predominant paradigm of free-form natural language messaging between agents, where roles encode communication norms as standardized operating procedures, and the emergent limitations such as cascading hallucinations in naive chaining. It contrasts with designs that impose more rigid message structures or metadata formats to coordinate the communication of instructions and intermediate results while reducing ambiguity.
2. Embedding-based Communication and Belief State Transfer: Investigates the emerging communication regimes that transmit raw hidden representations or "belief states" rather than discrete tokens. This approach, termed "cipher" communication, bypasses the token-sampling step to mitigate information loss and conveys continuous semantic distributions. It significantly enhances the fidelity and density of information exchanged among agents, yielding higher performance with little to no modification of model weights.
3. Communication Interplay with the Self-Adaptive and Environmental Loop: Focuses on the communication design as a control loop where agents share informative observations, plan adjustments, and execute adaptive responses within dynamic environments. Exploring communication protocols that enhance context awareness and prompt direct, clear information exchange mechanisms to distribute tasks, modify plans, and maintain robust coordination when sensing the environment or coping with uncertainty.

### 3.2 Debate, Negotiation, and Consensus-Building Mechanisms
Description: This subsection details the interaction paradigms of several "social" behaviors, including adversarial debate, negotiation, voting with hierarchical structures, to resolve conflicts and achieve consensus. It details the workflows, from structured multi-round language model debates to markets-based mechanisms, and considers the theoretical and algorithmic conditions under which these interaction motifs produce reliable outputs by combining divergent knowledge and mitigating individual model fallacies.
1. Multi-Round Debate and Recursive Reasoning for Self-Consistency: Examines structured debate frameworks, where multiple agents "tit for tat" propose and challenge arguments, often in a distinguished judge's guide or via self-verification, to progressively refine answers and reduce hallucinations. It includes techniques for multi-agent debate with distinct roles (proposer, opponent, judge) and the incorporation of a judge model for resolving final decisions, improving factual validity and mathematical and strategic reasoning.
2. Negotiation, Game-Theoretic Interaction, and Mechanism Design: Analyzes strategic scenarios where agents have partially conflicting objectives or competing goals, including bargaining, price negotiation, and social dilemmas. It investigates the use of adaptive information modulation and incentivization by a pro-social "regulator" agent to enhance cooperation, and evaluates strategic behaviors such as pretending to be in distress to improve negotiation outcomes, shedding light on the theory of mind and rational reasoning.
3. Voting, Aggregation, and Resolution of Conflicting Viewpoints: Investigates mechanisms to reach a single final output from diverging agent opinions or models, including majority voting, dynamic aggregation of arguments, and aggregation of "argumentation frameworks" from social choice to preserve semantic property. It examines the role of meta-agents or roundtable debates to settle nuanced risks where the cognitive superiority of models can dominate, thus ensuring robust consensus that reflects the entire collectivity.

### 3.3 Structural Topologies and Dynamic Organizational Adaptation
Description: This section analyzes the static and dynamic organizational structures that coordinate LLM agents—from hierarchical task decomposition to flat, self-organizing, communication-driven systems. It explores the system-architecture-level trade-offs of centralized control vs. decentralized flexibility, scalability, and robustness, and highlights the organizational forms that shape information flow and the macro-level system behavior.
1. Hierarchical and Centralized Coordination Structure: Investigates layered top-down structures common in autonomous frameworks where a central planner or controller monitors agent status, decomposes tasks into subtasks, and coordinates activity, such as hierarchical planning with a deterministic centralized executor to aid the system’s "auto-organization". It explores patterns where a coordinator is the "team leader" agent and other expert agents receive task inputs to solve, and the trade-off of control vs. risk is studied.
2. Decentralized and Self-Organizing Collaborative Models: Delves into decentralized swarm systems where agent collectives dynamically form pairs or coalitions, changing communication topologies, in order to manage tasks. It examines the dynamic leadership organization in teams for efficient navigation where communication triggers emergency leadership, and self-organized agents that dynamically generate agents and multiply according to the problem complexity, enabling collaborative, scalable codebase generation or 590-agent-scale policy simulation without predefined Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs).
3. Organizational Structure and Emergent Governance Selection: Explores how organizational structures exist across a spectrum of stiffness, and how systems dynamically select or adapt organization models based on the task or changing operating environment and adapt the communication path or task-flow structure during the interaction. This includes setting up a self-organizing "auto-organization" communication hierarchy when subtasks require it and assembling dynamic teams based on humans factors of teammates, promoting "dynamic, ad hoc" teams in mixed human-agent settings.

### 3.4 Deliberative Group Dynamics and Collective Emergent Reasoning
Description: This subsection explores the emergent capabilities of multi-agent systems to implement a "society of mind"—where group interaction produces knowledge and cognition beyond what a single individual can achieve. It synthesizes the mechanisms for group problem-solving as decentralized solutions to the macro-level dynamics of consensus building and division of cognitive labor, including conditional and philosophical implications for reasoning as a distributed process.
1. The "Society of Minds" and Emergent Deliberation: Explores through the philosophy of Minsky's "society of mind" in multi-agent architectures, where group modules—not a single module—solve the process of complex reasoning via a higher-order function, allow effective division and layering of the reasoning processes. Discuss how the interaction of multiple agents proposing, critiquing, and refining solutions, with modules working in a harmonious way to break free from "Degeneration of Thought" traps and expand the solution space similarly to how a brain’s functional areas cooperate.
2. Social Dynamics and the Emergence of Collective Intelligence: Analyzes the macro-level interactions of agent societies in social simulations and generative agent environments, where the structure of influence diffuses, and civic/governance organizations can form. This includes how trust, a social contract, leadership as a norm, and the dynamics of "wisdom of partisan crowds" and "Artificial Leviathan" might emerge from a set of agents, all resembling the self-organization of social intelligence mechanisms seen in the actual world.
3. Agent-Agent Communication Entities Signals on System Robustness: Examines the functionality and degradation modes of emergent group interactions, such as unconstrained information pathway allowing the rapid spread of manipulated knowledge or a toxic cascade. It also covers the relevance of increasing inter-consistency via debate as a check for the potential fallibility of using multiple agents, therefore posing both a reward in terms of emergent output quality and robustness with systemic risk.

## 4 Collective Planning, Reasoning, and Learning in Multi-Agent Systems

It moves beyond static reasoning, focusing on high-level orchestration and the long-horizon adaptation of group behavior.
1. Task Decomposition and Resource Allocation: Discusses methods for transforming high-level goals into structured sequences of sub-tasks, including hierarchical task decomposition and allocation to different agents based on their specialization, and explores graph-based, dynamic, and adaptive planning strategies for the distribution of cognitive labor.
2. Shared Reasoning and Strategic Inter-Operation: Examines how multiple agents participate in more sophisticated reasoning, such as by combining information from any source in a debate-and-verification chain, or engaging in a principled process of generating a tree of intermediate solutions, and discusses emerging strategic behaviors that arise due to partially-aligned goals among agents.
3. Collaborative Learning and Feedback-driven Improvement: Covers mechanisms for system learning from the outcome of tasks through natural language feedback and self-evaluation, and contrasting these with the reinforcement learning based paradigms that fine-tune policy through environmental reward signals or use language model planners as controllers to orchestrate multi-round strategies.
4. Self-Evolution and Long-Term Adaptability: Introduces the role of explicit and structured processes for the system to generate its own new tools, form and refine internal policies from past experiences, and update organizational rules, which leads to the formation of collective long-term memory and evolving team dynamics rather than a static system.

## 5 Emergent Behaviors and Group Dynamics
Description: This section investigates the complex and often unpredictable patterns of behavior that emerge from the collective interaction of multiple autonomous agents, which are not explicitly programmed by the system designer. It systematically analyzes these emergent group dynamics, the social roles taken by agents, and the macro-level patterns of collaboration and competition and their consequences.
1. Formation of Societal Structures and Norms: Investigates the emergence of spontaneous phenomena like agent leadership, role specialization, and the formation of implicit social contracts or norms within a group, demonstrating how a decentralized collective self-organizes to a structure that was not present in initial conditions.
2. Collaborative and Competitive Dynamics in Teams: Analyzes the interaction motifs in cooperative, adversarial, and mixed-incentive environments, including patterns of cooperation, mutualism, information sharing, circular debate, and conflict where agents with slightly different tasks or incentives unintentionally create a functional balance in the group output.
3. Modeling and Simulating Human-like Social Phenomena: Describes the role of multi-agent systems for computational social science to recreate complex social environments, with individual agents representing citizens, departments of an organization, or economic agents, providing a tool to probe macro outcomes like the diffusion of opinions, financial market evolution, and urban dynamics.
4. The Conditions and the Limits of Collective Intelligence: Discusses the qualitative regimes where the interaction of a team of agents gives a super-linear increase in task capacity, but also defines the origin of emergent systemic degradation due to herding, echo-chambers, or escalating and "toxic" cascading of the logical errors of each individual.

### 5.1 Emergence of Societal Structures, Norms, and Conventions
Description: This subsection examines how decentralized collectives of LLM agents spontaneously self-organize into structured societies without explicit top-down programming. It focuses on the formation of implicit social contracts, leadership hierarchies, and normative systems that arise from repeated interactions and become stable, emergent properties of the group.
1. Spontaneous Role Specialization and Leadership: Explores how agents organically differentiate into specialized functions (e.g., coordinators, critics, task experts) rather than merely executing preset prompts. It analyzes the emergence of "hub" agents whose influence and informational connectivity make them de facto leaders, examining how this structural inequality impacts overall performance.
2. Formation of Group Norms and Implicit Social Contracts: Discusses how LLM agents dynamically develop and converge upon implicit behavioral standards, such as communication etiquette, resource sharing rules, or coordination heuristics. It details mechanisms for the evolution of such norms via iterative interactions, including the identification of "authoritative" norms that are consistently adopted and enforced by the collective.
3. Societal Phase Transitions and Tipping Points: Investigates how a decentralized group spontaneously transitions to a "society" when a key threshold of consensus or connectivity is crossed. This point often marks the emergence of superior group intelligence, whereas a lack of this threshold leads to radical dispersion, while excessive conformity can lead to homogenization or premature convergence.

### 5.2 Collaborative, Competitive, and Mixed-Incentive Dynamics
Description: This subsection systematically analyzes the elementary interaction motifs that define how LLM agents co-exist during a task, moving beyond the mere structure of the group to the behavioral patterns of cooperation, conflict, and mutualism in various incentive regimes.
1. Cooperation, Mutualism, and Spontaneous Information Sharing: Examines patterns in which agents act to enhance the team’s distributed outcome, including the organic division of labor, the ongoing process of knowledge pool formation, and pro-social information exchange. It also investigates the phenomenon of ad hoc teamwork, where agents must collaborate with previously unseen partners without prior coordination.
2. Debate, Adversarial Argumentation, and Competitive Dynamics: Analyzes the dynamics of in-opposition or contest-like structures—structured, adversarial, or circular debate—and the propagation of the respective ideas and solutions in such competitive environments. ## 7 Challenges, Limitations, and Open Research Frontiers
Description: This section synthesizes the state of the field, articulating the persistent technical, theoretical, and societal challenges that are limiting the broader adoption and continuous scalability of large language model-based multi-agent systems. It distills the open problems into a set of research axes that will guide the next generation of work in this domain.
1. Theoretical Underpinnings and System Verification: Highlights the lack of a principled mathematical and formal framework that can model the macro-behavior of a system, the emergent dynamics and potential convergence, and the ability to deadlock, thereby allowing for formal guarantees of stability, of a multi-agent system.
2. Scalability, Efficiency, and the Population Challenge: Addresses the specific constraints on system scale, including the exploding computational and financial cost when more than a few participants take part, and argues for an urgent need for new methods for sparse communication, model compression, and algorithmic caching to deploy teams of thousands or even millions.
3. Continuous Evolution and the Static Systems Dilemma: Diminishes the gap between static agents defined at construction time and the promise of an organic system that can change its internal architecture, reproduce and update itself based on the task, and adapt to non-stationary environments. This includes end-to-end training and persistent development.
4. Governance, Accountability, and Interoperability: Explores the layered issue of managing a human-agent organization, including the need for standardized communication and discovery protocols for agents to seamlessly operate across different platforms, and for parsing errors to not be trusted in *decision provenance*.
5. The Design of Fundamental Principles of Collective Intelligence: Recognizes the lack of consensus on the design principles that govern what kinds of tasks require massive participation, and the role of heterogeneous models, new cooperation mechanisms inspired by social and ecological systems, and the move toward an "agent ecosystem" that includes a broader set of failure models, and multi-agent modalities.

### 7.1 Theoretical Underpinnings and Formal Verification
Description: This subsection examines the fundamental lack of formal mathematical frameworks capable of modeling, analyzing, and verifying the macro-level behavior of LLM-based multi-agent systems. It addresses the challenges of predicting emergent dynamics, ensuring convergence, and reasoning about equilibrium states, while highlighting the need for guarantees on system stability, termination, and safety that are largely absent in current black-box, stochastic agent architectures.
1. Formal Models for Emergent Dynamics and Convergence: The difficulty of establishing methodological tools (including formal languages, model checking, and theorem proving adapted from classical distributed systems and game theory) to reason about emergent collective behavior, and the need to develop formal guarantees of convergence and security guarantees from local agent interactions in the presence of stochastic socio-linguistic generation processes.
2. Rigorous Verification via Model Checking and Formal Verification: The difficulty of extending classical formal verification methods to the state spaces of arbitrarily large and heterogeneous LLM-driven societies, addressing the state-space explosion problem, and needing a principled account of verifiability, with methods for abstracting and reducing model complexity while preserving the informational, observation, and epistemic content of different strategies and the subtleties of information in multi-agent systems.
3. Reasoning Limits and Formal Impossibility Results: The theoretical impossibility results that bound the capacity of a group of learners and agents to acquire or amalgamate knowledge in heterogeneous environments, including the emergence of ‘impossibility of collective intelligence’ due to incomparability of environments and context, and the need of in-depth analyses of the computationally upper bounds on mechanisms such as plurality, deliberation, and consensus in the presence of multiple individuals.

### 7.2 Scalability, Efficiency, and Computational Feasibility
Description: This subsection addresses the fundamental limitations that restrict current agent systems to small-scale experiments, focusing on the O(n²) communication and computation costs, context window limits, and economic costs of massively parallel LLM interactions. It investigates novel methods and architectural paradigms such as sparse communication, population-based parameter sharing, and GPU-accelerated environments for high-performance, large-scale simulation.
1. Scaling Multi-Agent Populations Through Efficient Communication and Coordination: Paradigms and algorithms (e.g., dynamic, sparse, hierarchical and small-world communication topologies) for reducing the project of an O(N²) communication overhead, algorithmic caching, and multi-phase strategies that allow effective LLM agent collaboration at scale, with an emphasis on key findings that suitable organisation can, at scale, outperform and have better performance than monolithic systems.
2. Computation, Memory, and Economic Feasibility of Agent Societies: The urgent infrastructure requirement for the modelling of a large number of interacting components, including memory and context-view management and the importance of sparse interaction patterns, and the new framework for running simulation up to millions of agents (e.g., GPU-based, state-optimised frameworks) to realise simulation timescales of hours, reducing the cost by adopting more efficient sampling strategies with strong performance.
3. Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning and Constructing Large-Scale Optimization: A combined view of the complexities and scaling challenges that the field of multi-agent reinforcement learning (and learning across a large number of agents) brings to large-scale natural language agent systems, including the pressing need for a move from resource-intensive, to methods that are based on a smaller set of parameters or are large-scale efficient, such as( RL-based adaptive decision-making processes in populations and learning over a population of the agents).
4. Multi-Agent Operating Systems: OS and Middleware for Large-Scale Agents: Working towards the use of LLM-based agents as applications and for the efficient use of resources, through paradigm shifts such as LLM as OS, and data-flow based scheduling, to mitigate the high costs of repeatedly querying model and parse, and facilitate a system ecosystem that is capable of scaling to millions of participants.

### 7.3 Continuous Evolution, Learning, and Dynamic Reconfiguration
Description: This section explores the static nature of current LLM-based multi-agent systems compared to dynamic environments. It surveys mechanisms for end-to-end learning, self-evolving agents, open-endedness, and self-organising structures to enable long-term autonomy and group-level learning. It discusses adaptation at the systems level, including the ability of the system to evolve its own architectural composition and processes during the task.
1. Self-Evolving Agents, Self-Exploration, and Multi-Generation Training: Methods for self-evolving agents and automatic policy/task update from past memory and new interactions in a training framework that includes feedback and self-evolution from a triple of diverse environments, resources and direct interaction, to allow for both expert-designed and auto-generated multi-agent systems and that shift from the static "train-and-deploy" paradigm to one of perpetual, dynamic evolution of member-agent policies.
2. Automatic Generation, Evolution, and Modern Architectures of an Agent Team: Generation methods, including evolutionary algorithms, that use evolutionary operations (mutation, crossover, selection) to automatically produce a success of multiple and varied expert agents from a basic existing architecture, and the associated need for self-enhancement across the population and across generations, including using self-evaluation and LLM-as-a-judge architectures.
3. Self-Adaptation and Learning of an Emergent Organization: The formalization and methods for the design of self-adaptive organizational rules for the changing of the agent team structure and its internal norms in real-time, including concepts from the MAPE-K reference model and the principles of the online learning of a new policy (e.g., by modulating information access) to promote cooperative and agent-level decision actions.
4. Lifelong, Non-Episodic Environments and Long-Horizon Adaptation: The challenges and possibilities for a system to learn in a non-episodic situation (i.e., one that does not repeat or is under the environment) and continuous adaptation to the environment and the population, including the introduction of modifications to the evolutionary process for the team to remain adaptive and evolving in presence of non-stationary worlds and an API ecosystem.

### 7.4 Governance, Trust, and Socio-Technical System Design
Description: This subsection navigates the complex interplay of governing LLM-agent populations, the need for robust and trustworthy, and the interoperability and safety standards required to orchestrate a functional, human-machine collaborative environment. 1. Trust in AI, Agency, and Reliability: New challenge to and consideration of the question of Trust on the emergent properties of LLM-agents, why it is critical to close the gap between traditional definitions of "intelligent agents" and LLM-based agents for the purposes of authentication and authorization, and the need to systematically evaluate human trust toward the decisions and "agency" of the system.
2. Governance, Policy, and the Need for Standardization and Large-Scale “Standard Problem”: The legal and political problem of governing a multi-agent system, from the development of community-level standards of communication, on system and web-level infrastructure, for a transparent and verifiable agent, along with the “standard problem” of establishing an agent-agent contract for collaboration in open and heterogeneous systems (between agents with different levels of capability), to provide minimal interoperability, and ensure the autonomy of such constituents.
3. Combining humans and AI-Systems: From Autonomy to Interventions and Safety: Key findings on the dynamic balance between the degree of human oversight and autonomous of the AI assistant, evaluating how autonomy and alignment for the system can be jointly tuned, and integrating the human-in-the-loop and human-agent structures (e.g., reflection) to ensure a safety envelope on actions. The models of autonomy (hierarchical review, pre-hoc and post-hoc) are considered, and the design of a verifier and moderator to constrain agent.
4. This governs the creation of self-organized social order and norms, the resolution of conflict and cooperation (e.g., negotiation, mediation, etc.), and the cost of “social deliberation” and adopting social contracts with a large set of rule with frequent decision-making in resource-limited settings.

### 7.6 Security, Privacy, and Robustness of Interacting Agent Collectives
Description: This subsection focuses on the new security, alignment, and privacy attack surfaces introduced by the division of a system into multiple interacting agents, each a potential attack vector. It deals with the threat of cascading errors, hallutination and policy-of-mind, and collision of adversarial or bad agents. It additionally discusses the critical need for privacy as in distributed learning protocols, and the system and collective level and approach to confer robustness to attack.
1. Safety of the Agent Group and Attack and Failure Analysis: The increase in attack surface and security vulnerabilities due to multiple agents, and a novel typology of cascading security of the multi-agent workflow, including security through compromised data (prompt injection), module and agent permissions, and the potential to propagate a single- point contamination and/or (jailbreak) on a full system, by design.
2. Hallucination and Misalignment Cascades/Errors in a System: The contemporary risk of a propagating logical ‘hallucination’ when an agent's false premise is shared as a received truth across the agent network, and the need for new guarantees to detect, to mitigate by use of robust, external or strong diversity-promoting inference, and avoid the “groupthink” (false consensus) where accuracy increases confidence but not correctness or reduces failure is fundamental.
3. Collective Alignment and Safe/Robust system design: A discussion of the complexity of definition and ensuring safe alignment in the context of a system with multiple, possibly conflicting goals for several parties, leading to a safety-critical condition, and criteria to the design of control mechanisms, “verifier-in-the-loop”, and dynamic, and adaptive monitoring systems to ensure robust and secure coordination under a potential adversarial setting.
4. Privacy in Multi-Agent Architectures for Distributed and Collective Data: The lack of privacy-preserving computation and training for LLM multi-agent systems in a collaborative process; the need for training and deployment of a distributed set of agents that can guarantee the privacy and security of learner sets, and conflicts between an agent's private values and the utility in a multi-lateral and societal context, using techniques such as local training, and alignment.
