### 2.1 Historical Evolution: From Parametric-Only Architectures to Semi-Parametric Systems  
Description: This subsection traces the architectural lineage of large language models, moving from purely parametric systems toward semi-parametric designs that integrate non-parametric memory. It establishes the historical groundwork by examining the limitations of parametric-only models and the early prototypes of retrieval-augmented architectures. The progression demonstrates how the field evolved from monolithic neural networks to hybrid systems that combine parametric knowledge with external, non-parametric knowledge stores, setting the stage for the retrieval-augmented generation paradigm.
1. The Rise of Parametric-Only Language Models: A critical analysis of how pre-trained language models like GPT and T5 came to rely exclusively on fixed, implicit world knowledge encoded within their parameters during training, presenting their strengths and the subsequent challenges of knowledge staleness, hallucination, and their inability to incorporate verifiable, real-time information.
2. The Emergence of Non-Parametric Memory and Early Retrieval-Augmented Models: A historical account of early attempts to introduce explicit, non-parametric memory architectures, including k-nearest-neighbor language models and Memory Networks, which drew connections between outputs and external data points or small, episodic memory stores, thereby transitioning from fully-parametric to semi-parametric systems.
3. The Growth of Large-Scale Retrieval-Augmented Language Models: An analysis of the pivotal narrowing of scale and scope that moved small memory stores to massive, dense vector indexes of the open web, and how the major steps, such as REALM, DPR, and RAG, established the general-purpose, pre-trained retriever-generator architecture for knowledge-intensive NLP tasks, creating the conceptual precursor to modern RAG frameworks.
4. Foundational Insights from Early Systems: A summary of the key lessons taken from these initial methodologies, such as the benefits of grounding text generation in retrieved passages to reduce hallucinations and improve truthfulness, as well as the early cost of the integration and optimization of multiple, distinct components (retriever and generator), defining the research agenda for contemporary RAG.

### 2.2 The Motivational Nexus: Addressing Core Limitations of Large Language Models  
Description: This subsection provides a systematic categorization of the principled motivations that have driven the adoption of retrieval augmentation, linking these to the specific failure modes of fine-tuned and parametric-only LLMs. It explores known issues such as hallucination, outdated world models, and the absence of verifiability, and articulates how they act as a catalyst for the technical innovations discussed in the subsequent parts of the survey.
1. Alleviating Hallucination and Increasing Truthfulness: A breakdown of the persistent task of parametric models generating plausible yet inaccurate statements and the ways in which access to retrieved evidence provides a context-bound grounding that can be factually checked and used to increase faithfulness and correctness.
2. The Knowledge Refreshing and Up-Dateability Constraint: A survey of the problem of knowledge stability, reflecting on the difficulties of updating factual information in a model post-training period, and a description of how an external, mutable knowledge store allows flexible and agile updates without the need for continual expensive retraining.
3. Enabling Domain-Specific and Real-Time Adaptation: A review of how retrieval facilitates the grounding of LLMs in specialized domains (eQA for finance, legal, or medical) as well as specific knowledge bases, without retraining; this directly bypasses the challenges of low-resource or unique domains and allows for the dynamic reintroduction of private or internal knowledge.
4. Fostering Interpretability and Attribution: An exploration of how the very nature of the retrieval-augmented workflow, by concretizing the information flow, provides a direct path for citations, references, and explainability of the final content, addressing a fundamental need for trustworthiness in high-stakes fields.

### 2.3 The Evolutionary Taxonomy of Retrieval-Augmented Paradigms  
Description: In this subsection, a formal taxonomy is developed to organize the diverse landscape of RAG design, structured by the nature and timing of the interaction between the retrieval and generative modules. This provides a framework to categorize the evolution from basic, single-step methods to advanced multi-stage and adaptive ones. This taxonomy clarifies the field's progress from simple data inclusion to complex, system-level orchestration and identifies future potential in modular approaches.
1. The Early Paradigms: Naive and Single-Step Approaches: This point addresses the foundation of `Retrieve-Read` and the standard single-shot RAG approach, exploring how naive, static pipelines retrieve a set of documents and concatenate them into the prompt with minimal interaction, discussing the benefits for simpler tasks but also highlighting their limitations in processing complex user requirements.
2. The Advanced Paradigms: Iterative and Recursive Retrieval: This designs the progression to iterative and recursive systems, where a dynamic retrieval–generation interaction drives an information acquisition process across multiple rounds. The survey covers methods such as `Iter-RetGen` and `IRCoT` which interleave reasoning steps with retrieval, allowing for the synthesis of inherent information over multi-hop reasoning and the ability to modify questions based on inherent knowledge.
3. The Rise of Modular and Agentic Systems: The current SOTA step, in which RAG is no longer a simple two-model but a flexible agentic framework where the LLM acts as a controller. This discusses how systems use adaptive retrieval triggers, planning, and tool usage to decide what, when, and how to retrieve, as well as introduce the concept of modular composition where modules can be formally and independently re-combined and orchestrated, including CRAG, Self-RAG, and deep-thinking agents.
4. A Generalized Design Framework: Toward a Unified Taxonomy: Synthesis of a holistic spectrum of proposed RAG is created to identify the paradigm from `naiveRAG` to `AdvancedRAG` and `ModularRAG`. It defines the central axes of comparison (when to retrieve, how to model, and in which granularity the augmentation) to allow a conceptual synthesis that works as a conceptual map for the reminder of the survey, and for the contextualization of specific techniques.

### 2.4 Delineation from Adjacent Techniques for Knowledge Integration  
Description: This subsection formally positions the retrieval-augmented generation within the broader context of machine learning methods for dynamic knowledge injection. It clarifies the analogous goals, strategic differences, and complementary natures of RAG against fine-tuning, continual learning, and model editing, scrutinizing each possibility on key trade-offs like parameter complexity, required data, and operation ability.
1. Contrasting RAG and Parameter-Efficient/Full Fine-Tuning: A comparative analysis on the boundary of the approaches, with the fundamental "weights-tuning" vs. "context-tuning" distinction. It reviews the trade-offs about parameter efficiency (zero-change vs. altered per task) and memory in compute and both performance and cost of direct adaptation, as well as on how to move between "parametric" and "semi-parametric" boundaries.
2. The Difference Between RAG and Continual Learning: An examination of RAG’s ability to update a knowledge index in contrast to the idea of updating the model weights. This section focuses on cumulative update mechanisms, the issue of catastrophic forgetting; it points out how RAG bypasses the risk of knowledge overwriting and serves as a complementary formula for integrating awareness of the world.
3. A Complementary Look at Model Editing: Address strategies of RAG and traditional knowledge injection vs. surgical knowledge editing methods. It highlights deep-learning practice of `locate and edit`, showing that, while model editing modifies the weight complexly, RAG provides a non-invasive path to achieve the desired outcome; it requires a high-engineering footprint, updates of knowledge are lighter and targeted, and so cannot substitute but can be integrated.
4. Selecting for a Needs-Based Regime: A broader meta-perspective: Analysis of the strategic choice of an engineering mechanism for integrating knowledge, which is only based on the task type. Creation of a decision-tree of when to use RAG, fine-tuning, or post-editing. Use of hybrid and iterative strategies, per case (long-tail, private data, real-time fact) and one primary trade-off in cost and flexibility combined with the interpolation of both learning and extraction.

## 3 The Retrieval-Centric Engine: Indexing, Query Processing, and Search Strategies
Description: This section provides a granular treatment of the retrieval subsystem, which is the principal mechanism for drawing upon external evidence. It examines the entire sequence of operations regarding the construction of a usable knowledge base, from the raw data preparation to the representation of queries and documents for semantic matching, and concludes with the strategic algorithms that form the search strategy.
1. Construction of Storage Structures and Corpus Indexing Techniques: A taxonomy of data segmentation strategies, including fixed-size windows, sentence-based layouts, semantic chunking, and considerations for structured data types; discussion centers on how granularity affects the recall and context utilization of downstream tasks and examines the use of hierarchical index design, knowledge graphs, and relational structures to mitigate trade-offs between search efficiency and context information density.
2. Embedding Models and Document Semantic Representation: An analysis of methods for mapping queries and documents from heterogeneous sources into a distributed space of dense vectors, and the design of bi-encoder and cross-encoders for the fusion of information, with a focus on training retrieval-targeted language models.
3. Search Mechanisms: From Sparse to Dense to Generative Paradigms: A comparison of lexical (e.g., best matching 25) and dense semantic retrieval, covering approximate nearest neighbor search, vector-indexing mechanisms (e.g., hierarchical navigable small world), and the recent paradigm of fully generative retrieval, where documents are decoded directly in a sequence-to-sequence manner or via learned document identifiers to eliminate the need for complex external search structures.
4. Integration of Retriever and Generator Quality: A rigorous treatment of optimization techniques for the retriever to align with generation utility, including training via contrastive loss, hard negative mining, and end-to-end differentiation through the entire pipeline as well as the role of pre-trained language models as improved semantic encoders for indexing and retrieval.

### 3.1 Corpus Construction, Indexing Structures, and Chunking Strategies
Description: This subsection provides a comprehensive examination of the foundational layer of the retrieval subsystem, focusing on the critical decisions and techniques involved in transforming raw corpora into a structured, searchable knowledge base. It presents a taxonomy of chunking methodologies and discusses how indexing design choices at varying granularities influence the trade-off between retrieval recall and the contextual density available to the downstream generator, including the integration of relational structures for complex knowledge.
1. Granularity-Aware Chunking Paradigms: A detailed breakdown of text segmentation strategies, including fixed-size windowing, sentence-based boundaries, and semantic or structure-aware chunking. The description analyzes how each method's granularity affects retrieval precision and the contextual sufficiency of the returned passages, with particular emphasis on recent findings that structured, element-based chunking enhances RAG performance in document-heavy domains.
2. Hierarchical and Multi-Level Index Architectures: An examination of recursive index designs and multi-tiered data structures that cluster information based on summarization or information density. This subsection highlights the use of Depth-First Search-based querying mechanisms, such as those in HIRO, which reduce the context delivered to the LLM without loss of information, and compares these with flat database partitions and the Multiple Partitions approach (e.g., M-RAG) for reducing noise and focus on crucial memories.
3. Knowledge Graphs, Semantic Wikis, and Relational Structures: An analysis of integrating structured data models to enrich the retrieval context. This covers techniques for connecting text chunks via relational dependencies and knowledge graph edges, the use of meta-knowledge summaries for clusters of documents, and the strategic use of distant supervision signals to prepare knowledge bases for high-precision retrieval.
4. Data Modification, Quality Control, and Pipeline Integration: A discussion of the preprocessing and augmentation steps vital for indexing, including the handling of semi-structured and heterogeneous formats. It explores how document parsers convert complex data into vectorizable formats and the role of synthetic Question-Answer pair generation to augment queries and documents, thereby enabling user-query personalization and in-depth knowledge-base traversal.

### 3.2 Query Processing, Reformulation, and Semantic Representation

1. Query Rewriting and Multi-Perspective Reformulation: An investigation into methods that refine and expand the original query prior to retrieval. 2. Bi-Encoders, Cross-Encoders, and Dense Retrieval Architectures: An analysis of representation learning for query-document semantic matching. The focus is on the design and training of bi-encoders for scalable embedding and cross-encoders for high-fidelity interaction modeling, and the investigation into how representation models embed long documents, including positional biases and the degradation of information from the middle of the input sequence.
3. Handling Multilingual and Cross-Dialect Inquiries: An exploration into the challenges of mapping queries and documents from varying languages and dialects into a shared space of dense vectors, surveying dense retrieval performance across varying linguistic contexts as well as the adaptation of existing models for low-resource and dialect-heavy settings.

Description: This subsection provides an analytical overview of the algorithms and structural paradigms underpinning the search strategies in retrieval systems. ### 3.4 Retriever-Generator Alignment and Co-Optimization Strategies

Intermediate and Fusion-in-Decoder Paradigms: A discussion of dedicated architecture that integrates retrieved information through cross-attention, allowing the decoder to integrate evidence over multiple sources of documents, contrasts the encoder-decoder and decoder-only transformers for more efficient multi-document synthesis.

### 4.1 Context Integration Strategies and the Input Pipeline
Description: This subsection examines the fundamental architectural decisions governing how retrieved information enters the generative model. The discussion explains the importance of studying these design choices as they fundamentally shape the capacity of the system to reason over retrieved knowledge.
1. Static Concatenation and Prompt-Based Injection: An examination of the foundational retrieve-then-read paradigm, where retrieved documents are simply prepended or appended to the input prompt for a frozen language model. This includes analysis of how context ordering and ranking effects, similar to "lost in the middle" phenomena, affect generation quality and how these findings guide systematic approaches for effective document arrangement.
2. Dynamic Contextual Triggering: An analysis of adaptive strategies that decide whether retrieval is necessary for a specific query. This framework assesses query clarity and model confidence prior to retrieval, incorporating selective augmentation mechanisms to avoid unnecessary computational overhead while improving efficiency and accuracy by avoiding harmful retrieval actions.
3. Query Rewriting and Reformulation within the Context Pipeline: A focus on methods that transform or augment the user query to bridge the lexical and semantic gap between the original input and the external knowledge sources. 4. Contextual Placement and Key-Value Cache Efficiency: A study of the impact of document placement on inference-time speed and model quality. This includes a discussion of the trade-offs between prepending versus appending retrieved documents in the context within the context of augmented models, and an evaluation of how special system prompt tokens and low-rank adaptation can be used to maintain answer quality while achieving significant decoding speedups.

### 4.2 Fusion-in-Decoder and Cross-Attention Architectures for Multi-Document Synthesis
Description: This subsection addresses model-level techniques for reasoning over information extracted from multiple sources. Multi-Document Encoding and Cross-Attention Mechanisms: An overview of a combination of dedicated cross-attention mechanisms and Encoder-Decoder models that provide an alternative to the simple concatenation by processing each retrieved document independently and fusing the information in the decoder. ### 4.3 Adaptive Retrieval Planning and Dynamic Routing
Description: This subsection investigates decision-making mechanisms that govern retrieval planning--when to retrieve, how many times to retrieve, and how to orchestrate multiple retrieval actions. 1. Complexity-Based Routing and Query Assessment: An analysis of adaptive frameworks that use a classifier to estimate query complexity and route the task to the appropriate strategy from a set of choices (e.g., no retrieval, single-step retrieval, or multi-step retrieval). 2. Gating and Self-Aware Retrieval Triggers: A study of methods that train orphan classifiers to evaluate the model’s internal state of knowledge and the quality of retrieved context to determine the need for external knowledge. A key component is the high utility of assessing the necessity of retrieval and enhancing the robustness of the system by correcting irrelevant or noisy retrieved information before final answer generation.
3. Reinforcement Learning for Adaptive Interaction and Tool Use: An exploration of the use of reinforcement learning (RL) to train a policy for using retrieval-based tools. This includes frameworks that use the LM itself to directly rank or judge the relevance, and incorporates reinforcement learning to learn optimal document orderings or to control a multi-agent system navigating database partitions to optimize the generation task.

## 5 Training Paradigms and Adaptation for Augmented Models
Description: This section explains the supervised and unsupervised learning strategies that can be used to train the constituent models of a retrieval-augmented system, either jointly or individually, to find optimal parameterization for the target tasks. The overall training pipeline encompasses all stages from foundational pre-training, through fewer-shot and prompting the implementation, towards multi-objective optimization and active learning.
1. Joint Optimization of Retriever and Re-Generator: A detail of end-to-end training using task-specific losses, illustrating how a system can propagate gradients from the generator to the retriever concerning discrete retrieval decisions, and it reviews the differentiability relaxation that occur within dense and generative retrieval context.
2. ### 6.1 Adversarial Threats via Knowledge Base Poisoning and Backdoor Injection
Description: This subsection examines attacks that exploit the openness of RAG's external knowledge store, where adversaries manipulate the retrieval corpus itself to compromise the overall system. It details attack vectors including corpus poisoning, targeted misinformation dissemination, and Trojan or backdoor triggers embedded within retrieved documents.
1. **Corpus Poisoning Attacks for Target Answer Manipulation:** Formalizes knowledge poisoning as an optimization problem, where an attacker injects crafted adversarial passages into an external database (e.g., with millions of documents) to force a specific target answer for a given question; discusses the high success rates and insufficient defense of current systems in black-box and white-box settings.
2. **Covert Misinformation Dissemination and Backdoor Triggers:** Analyzes attacks that leverage grammar errors or specific trigger phrases (e.g., TrojanRAG, backdoor attacks on dense passage retrievers) to covertly retrieve attacker-specified passages containing misinformation, hate speech, or advertisements, while maintaining normal behavior for error-free or standard queries.
3. **Exploiting the Open-Domain Architecture for Malicious Injection:** Investigates the remote attack surface of RAG, where adversaries with no knowledge of the user queries, knowledge base data, or model parameters can exploit the retriever and uploaded crafted content to subvert the generation, mirroring real-world scenarios like user-contributed data pools.

### 6.2 Vulnerabilities Arising from Context Injection and Prompt Manipulation
